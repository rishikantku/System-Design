Host Leader · 60 minutes

A hiring leader who can tell you about teams, engineering culture and the future of LinkedIn. The focus is a deep dive into your background and your leadership skills, with concrete examples of past projects where you had challenges, made decisions, and accomplished something.

What they evaluate

  • Communication — how you communicate and influence technical decisions within your team and across an organisation
  • Culture — how you have built a team culture; does it involve taking intelligent risks and learning from mistakes?
  • Influence — how you identify opportunities for improvement (technology or process) and get buy-in across the organisation or company
  • Mentorship — your mentorship style; how you grow and mentor engineers at team level or larger
  • Conflict — resolving conflicts and difficult situations constructively

What to expect

  • Concrete examples, not philosophy
  • Challenges, decisions, accomplishments

How to run it

  • Bring specific projects
  • Be ready to go deep on your own background
Source: LinkedIn recruiter pack — "Staff SI Onsite Prep - CWAI", Systems & Infrastructure · received 21 September 2026

The platform thesis — spotting the problem nobody owned · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

Three product teams designed their own per-customer isolation in one week; I wrote a one-page note to my VP asking to pause that until we decided what belonged in the platform, and was asked to lead the design.

The beats, in order

  1. About a month into Isolated Cloud, sat through design reviews from three product teams in the same week.
  2. Jira was planning its own per-customer stack with routing; Confluence something different; a third team waiting to copy whichever looked better.
  3. "We were not building one Isolated Cloud. We were about to build five or six."
  4. Wrote a one-page note to the VP that weekend; the ask was to pause product-level isolation designs until the platform boundary was decided.
  5. Listed everything a service does differently for an isolated tenant and marked each item with the Jira and Confluence leads: product-specific or same for everyone.
  6. Almost everything was the same for everyone → the principle: isolation is a platform capability, not a product feature.

Migrating ~178 teams onto the platform · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

I planned dependencies, not migrations: a three-question form rebuilt the dependency map, tiers L0–L3 (plus L2.5) sequenced the work, and an onboarding tool made adoption cheaper than resistance.

The beats, in order

  1. The service catalogue was wrong, so every team got a short form: what do you call, what calls you, do you send data outside Atlassian.
  2. Tiers: L0 identity and edge, L1 shared platform, L2 the products first customers needed, L3 the tail, plus L2.5 inserted during planning to avoid renumbering dashboards and runbooks.
  3. Only the dependency graph was a hard constraint; priority, traffic, risk and team readiness shaped the rest.
  4. Onboarding tool in five steps: readiness check, generated shard descriptor, encrypted transfer, validation, status to a shared dashboard.
  5. Status was computed by the tool, never self-reported — self-reported status was always greener than reality.
  6. Onboarding went from a few weeks with hands-on help to a few days on their own.
  7. Phase two (the tail) took as long as phase one: it was an adoption problem, not an engineering one.

The SRE lead: should an unhealthy shard fall back to commercial? · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

A fair reliability instinct that would have been a compliance breach; the answer was no fallback anywhere, and reliability from replicas instead.

The beats, in order

  1. Raised in an architecture review: if the isolated shard is unhealthy, send the tenant to commercial — some service beats none.
  2. In a normal system that is good practice. Here it would serve an isolated customer from shared infrastructure.
  3. An outage is a reliability problem; serving from shared infrastructure is a compliance failure.
  4. Decision: fail closed everywhere, and buy availability with replicas inside the shard, not an escape route.
  5. Backed by three independent locks and an alert whose expected value is zero.

The shard-replacement ordering bug · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

We invalidated the cache before the repository update landed, so it refilled with stale placement and some requests reached a shard being retired; the fix was the ordering rule plus an alert.

The beats, in order

  1. Shard replacement during maintenance: placement updated and cache invalidated, but in the wrong order.
  2. The cache refilled from the old placement before the repository update landed.
  3. Fix: update the shard configuration repository first, then invalidate the cache, then route new traffic.
  4. Added an alert for any request reaching a retired shard.
  5. Both incidents traced to the same root: cache invalidation designed in late.

Moving live customers out of shared stores · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

Copy then switch, with one rule repeated in every review — a tenant has one home at a time — because dual-write kept isolated tenants wired to commercial and starting fresh pushed our risk onto the customer.

The beats, in order

  1. Three options weighed: start fresh, dual-write, or copy then switch.
  2. Start fresh pushed years of history and audit trails onto the customer; dual-write changed every write path and made both environments look authoritative.
  3. Chosen: copy then switch. Bulk copy, catch-up runs, short freeze, validation (ownership, counts, checksums), switch, rollback window, delete and verify.
  4. Service owners wrote the tenant-scoped extraction because only they knew their schema; the platform ran the encrypted transfer and the ownership check.
  5. The rollback conversation happened with each customer before cutover, not during an incident.

The GraphQL gateway and DataLoader · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

The gateway was every edge of the dependency map at once, and DataLoader silently lost tenant context on the batch thread where the default endpoint was commercial — caught because we checked where every downstream call went, not just what came back.

The beats, in order

  1. Every other service is one box on the dependency map; the gateway is every edge.
  2. Ran the gateway inside each isolated environment because query results are customer data.
  3. Hydration: the gateway makes the call, not the service, so per-service readiness checks could not see it — extended the check to the hydration graph, failing composition at build time.
  4. DataLoader batches ran on another thread without tenant context; the default endpoint was commercial — a fallback hidden in a library.
  5. Fixes: capture context into the loader at creation, fail closed without it, and make loaders strictly per request.
  6. Accepted slightly less batching for the guarantee.

Running it in production · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

Provisioning went from about two days to around five hours by making every step idempotent and resumable, and the signal that caught most problems early was egress denials.

The beats, in order

  1. Declarative provisioning: every step "make sure this exists and matches", so a failure resumes instead of duplicating.
  2. Quota check first, after a fresh account hit a database-instance limit mid-deploy; register last, so a half-built environment can never take traffic.
  3. Four signals watched: shard health, latency and errors per tenant and product, migration progress per team, egress denials.
  4. Egress denials were expected to be a compliance metric and became the best early warning.
  5. Rollout: commercial, then Atlassian-owned environments, then canaries with a bake, then everyone within change windows.

Business impact · 90-second storyyours
Your experience

Regulated customers who could not use our cloud at all could move, starting with a large bank, and isolation became a platform capability instead of 178 separate redesigns.

The beats, in order

  1. These customers' compliance teams would not sign off on shared infrastructure, however good the logical isolation.
