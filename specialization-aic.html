Atlassian Isolated Cloud

A single-customer deployment of Atlassian Cloud: dedicated AWS accounts, a dedicated VPC, a dedicated domain, customer-managed keys, and egress blocked by default — operated by a control plane that is still shared. This page reconstructs the architecture from Atlassian’s public documentation, then builds the Staff-level engineering problems around it.

Read this first — the sourcing rule. Every claim on this page is tagged. Nothing is invented and presented as Atlassian’s internal design, because in a Staff+ interview a confidently wrong internal detail costs more than an admitted gap. The three tags are:

CONFIRMED Explicitly stated in Atlassian’s public documentation. Safe to assert. Sources are listed at the bottom.
INFERENCE Not documented, but a technically sound conclusion from what is. Say it as reasoning: “it isn’t documented, but it has to work roughly like this, because…”
SCENARIO A design problem constructed to be representative. Use it to demonstrate reasoning. Never present it as something Atlassian built or as something you personally did.

⚠ About your own contribution

This page teaches the system. It cannot tell you what you did. Wherever it names a possible ownership area, treat it as a prompt: use it only if it matches what you actually did, and where a metric is needed, use your real one or say you would have to check it. An interviewer who catches an inflated claim stops evaluating the architecture and starts evaluating you.

Part 1

The system

1

The simplest mental model

🏢 The analogy

A large hotel chain runs one building with a thousand rooms. Everyone shares the plumbing, the lifts and the front desk. It is efficient and it is how nearly every guest is served.

A few guests cannot stay there at all — not because the rooms are bad, but because their rules say they may not share a building. So the chain builds them a private house: their own walls, their own locks, their own plumbing.

What the chain does not duplicate is the company. The same reservations team, the same maintenance schedule, the same staff training and the same supply contracts run both. The house is private; the operation is shared.

That is Atlassian Isolated Cloud. The customer gets their own building. Atlassian keeps running one company.

What it actually is

CONFIRMED Atlassian describes Isolated Cloud as “a dedicated, single-tenant cloud environment managed by Atlassian for organizations with strict security, compliance, or data isolation requirements”, with “dedicated compute, storage, and networking — all separated and compartmentalized in a virtual private cloud (VPC)”.

CONFIRMED Each customer gets their own AWS Organizational Unit and AWS accounts, their own VPC, their own domain and edge, their own firewall, and customer-managed encryption keys for long-term persistent user-generated content. Data egress is blocked by default. It includes all Cloud Enterprise features plus Atlassian Guard Premium.

⚠ The terminology trap
“Single-tenant means one tenant.”

CONFIRMED Atlassian uses the phrase “single-customer architecture” rather than single-tenant, and the distinction is deliberate: one organization can run multiple tenants — separate sites for separate business units — inside their one Isolated Cloud environment.

So the isolation boundary is the customer, not the site. Getting this right in the first minute signals you have read the actual model rather than assumed it.

Why normal Atlassian Cloud is not enough for these customers

The honest answer is that it usually is technically enough, and that is not the point. Multi-tenant Atlassian Cloud is logically isolated and well-audited. The customers who need Isolated Cloud are blocked by something else:

  • A rule that forbids shared infrastructure, full stop — common in financial regulation, defence supply chains and critical national infrastructure. No amount of logical isolation satisfies a control that says dedicated.
  • Key custody. They need to hold the encryption keys themselves, in their own AWS account, so that revoking access is something they can do unilaterally.
  • Provable egress control. Not “we do not send your data anywhere” but an enforced boundary with an audit trail.
  • Noisy-neighbour risk — a genuine but secondary concern. Atlassian lists it as a benefit; it is rarely why anyone buys.

INFERENCE The commercial logic follows from that list: these were customers who could not move to Cloud at all, so they stayed on Data Center. Atlassian has announced Data Center support ends 28 March 2029. Isolated Cloud is the migration path for the segment that had none.

🎯 The one-paragraph answer

“Isolated Cloud is Atlassian Cloud deployed one customer at a time. Each customer gets their own AWS accounts, their own VPC, their own domain and edge, and their own encryption keys, with data egress blocked by default. What is not duplicated is the control plane — provisioning, deployment and fleet operations are shared across every isolated environment and the commercial cloud, because duplicating those per customer would mean running hundreds of copies of Atlassian rather than one. So the interesting engineering is all at the boundary: how you operate an environment you have deliberately given yourself limited access to, and how data crosses that boundary without carrying customer content with it.”

4

Control plane and data plane

Why the control plane is shared

The obvious question is why Atlassian did not isolate everything. The answer is arithmetic.

INFERENCE A fully isolated control plane means every customer gets their own deployment pipeline, their own orchestration, their own fleet tooling and their own operational staff model. At a hundred customers that is a hundred copies of the parts of Atlassian that are hardest to run, each drifting to a different version. A security patch would then be a hundred separate projects rather than one rollout.

Shared control plane means one pipeline, one set of tooling, one team that knows how deployment works. It is the decision that makes the product economically possible, and every hard problem on this page is downstream of it.

What crosses the boundary

CONFIRMED Atlassian classifies data and permits three handling modes across the egress point:

ModeMeaningDocumented examples
Transit-onlyUsed momentarily, then deletedDisplay name, account ID, locale, nickname — for rendering a support UI
PersistedStored in commercial cloud where genuinely neededEmail address, as the primary identifier on a support ticket
Temporary with TTLShort-lived, auto-deletedDocumented as short as 5 minutes

CONFIRMED The rule governing all of it: “non-UGC and non-personal data” — system metadata, approved configuration — may flow to the shared control plane. Personal data and user-generated content are obfuscated in transit unless there is a stated business need. Enforcement is by code-based policies in the Transit Policy Manager, with every field classified by a data taxonomy.

🔍 Why policy-as-code rather than review

INFERENCE Consider the alternative. If “do not egress customer content” is a guideline, then it is enforced by whoever is reviewing a pull request on a Friday afternoon, in a codebase where thousands of engineers add fields to messages that cross service boundaries. It will be violated, it will be violated silently, and the violation will be discovered by a customer.

Classifying every field and enforcing the classification in code converts that from a diligence problem into a build failure. It is the same instinct as putting a lint rule in CI rather than in a style guide — anything a human has to remember to enforce is eventually not enforced.

What happens when the control plane is unavailable

The most likely reliability question in the round, and the answer is a genuine architectural property worth reasoning out rather than reciting.

INFERENCE The isolated environment holds its own compute, database, storage, edge, and identity service. Nothing on the serving path requires a call to the commercial control plane. So a control-plane outage should leave customers fully able to log in and work. What stops is the things that are genuinely control-plane functions: provisioning new environments, rolling deployments, new site creation, billing, and reaching the shared Marketplace and Support Centre.

That is a strong property and it is worth saying why it holds rather than just asserting it: because identity was deliberately kept inside the boundary. Had authentication depended on id.atlassian.com, a control-plane outage would log every isolated customer out, and the isolation would have bought a shared failure domain instead of removing one.

Control plane is downStill worksStops working
INFERENCE throughout Login via customer IdP · Jira/Confluence/JSM · attachments · search · automation inside the boundary Provisioning · deployments · new sites · billing · Marketplace · support ticket linkage

INFERENCE The reverse case — a single isolated environment failing — should affect that customer only, since it is a separate AWS account with separate capacity. That is the blast-radius argument for the whole design, and it is the one genuinely reliability-positive consequence of isolation. Everything else about isolation makes reliability harder.

5

Network architecture

The confirmed network facts are: a dedicated VPC, a dedicated firewall, a dedicated edge and domain, egress denied by default, and CONFIRMED no support for point-to-point or site-to-site VPN from AWS “at this time”. Customer IP restrictions are done through Atlassian Guard IP allowlisting rather than at the network layer.

INBOUND user / browser | v ATLASSIAN GLOBAL EDGE DNS for <site>.<org>.atlassian-isolated.net | v INGRESS GATEWAY dedicated to this customer | +--> auth service → SSO redirect → customer IdP → User Context Token | v backend services inside the VPC OUTBOUND default: DENY service wants to call out | v Controlled & Monitored Egress | +-- is the destination an approved remote? no → blocked +-- is every field cleared by the taxonomy? no → obfuscated | v Isolated Context Gateway → commercial control plane or an admin-approved third party COMMERCIAL → ISOLATED Support Centre / Marketplace / id.atlassian.com | v ISOLATION GATEWAY only explicitly allowlisted API endpoints | everything else does not exist from outside v isolated services
Three distinct crossing points, each with a different enforcement model: endpoint allowlisting inbound, destination approval plus field-level obfuscation outbound, and the customer’s own IdP for user identity. A design where one gateway did all three would be far simpler and would have no way to express “this endpoint is reachable but this field is not”.

What happens if a service tries to send data somewhere it should not

CONFIRMED It does not reach the destination: egress is default-deny and enforced by policy rather than convention, and third-party automation traffic is blocked by default. INFERENCE The more interesting half is what should happen next, and it is worth volunteering because it shows operational thinking: a blocked egress attempt is a security signal, not just a failed request. It should be counted, attributed to a service, and alerted on when it is novel — because the first sign that a new feature quietly tried to phone home is a spike on that metric.

The interviewer challenge here: “Default-deny sounds good, but how do you stop it becoming a permanent ticket queue where every team asks for an exception and someone approves them all?”

Fair, and the answer is that the exception must be expensive in the right way: an egress rule should require naming the destination, the fields, the justification and an owner, and it should be reviewable and revocable by the customer’s admin rather than only by Atlassian — which CONFIRMED is what Admin Hub does for app remotes. A default-deny that only Atlassian can open is a weaker product than one the customer controls.

The endpoint and domain model

This section looks like trivia and is one of the highest-value things to know, because it is the concrete reason integrations break.

CONFIRMED Isolated Cloud customers get their own unique base domain, and the documented patterns are:

Why a tool cannot assume normal Cloud URLs

Three separate breakages, and they fail at different layers, which is what makes this a good interview answer rather than a detail:

  1. Pattern matching fails. Any code that validates a site with something like *.atlassian.net rejects a valid isolated site. This is extremely common in integrations, OAuth redirect-URI allowlists, and webhook receivers.
  2. Discovery fails. There is no single base domain, so anything that derives the API host from a constant — rather than taking it as configuration — points at the wrong place. The base URL has to become an input.
  3. Reachability fails. Even with the right URL, the endpoint may not be exposed through the Isolation Gateway at all, and the customer’s own network must allow the domain and its subdomains.
🎯 The architectural point to make

“The URL change is not cosmetic — it is the first place the isolation model becomes visible to everything outside it. Every integration that hardcoded a hostname was implicitly depending on multi-tenancy. Isolated Cloud turns the base URL into configuration, and that single change is why the app ecosystem needs a separate build rather than a flag.”

7

Identity and authentication

This is the most elegant part of the design and the best thing to bring up unprompted, because it contains an inversion most people do not expect.

🎯 The inversion

You would assume the isolated environment authenticates against Atlassian. It is the other way round. CONFIRMED The Isolated Cloud identity service acts as an OAuth 2.0 / OIDC identity provider for id.atlassian.com.

When an isolated user needs to reach a shared service, the commercial cloud federates inward to them. Credentials never leave the boundary; the commercial side receives a token with minimal claims.

Flow 1 — logging in to Jira or Confluence

user | v ATLASSIAN GLOBAL EDGE | v INGRESS GATEWAY (dedicated) | v AUTH SERVICE inspects headers, redirects to configured SSO | v CUSTOMER IdP SAML or OIDC, plus MFA SSO is the ONLY login method | v auth service validates the assertion | +--> mints a USER CONTEXT TOKEN (UCT) | v backend service all of the above happens INSIDE the isolation boundary. identity data, session state and auth processing never leave.

Flow 2 — reaching Support Centre or Marketplace

user goes to a commercial Atlassian service | v login service does a DOMAIN LOOKUP on the email address | v page offers the Isolated Cloud login option | v OAuth/OIDC authorization redirect --> the customer's ISOLATED OAuth server | user authenticates against their OWN IdP | <-- authorization code ---------------------+ | v commercial identity service exchanges code for an ID token | +--> claims: user id + isolated-cloud identifier ONLY +--> no personal data persisted in the commercial environment | v STATELESS SESSION, up to 24h, containing only opaque identifiers | v needs actual data? --> ISOLATION GATEWAY --> allowlisted endpoints only
The commercial side ends up holding an opaque identifier and a session, and nothing else. To render anything real it must ask back through the Isolation Gateway, where the set of answerable questions is fixed in advance.

Confirmed identity constraints worth knowing

  • CONFIRMED SSO is the only way users log in. There is no local password path.
  • CONFIRMED Google Workspace is not supported as an identity provider.
  • CONFIRMED Each isolated environment keeps a separate user database from commercial cloud. Same IdP support otherwise.
  • CONFIRMED SCIM provisioning is supported, but without Google or Azure AD sync.
  • CONFIRMED Inviting non-IdP users is not supported — every user must come from the directory.
  • CONFIRMED A customer with both isolated and commercial cloud must run separate organizations.
🔍 Why “no non-IdP invites” is an architecture decision

It looks like a missing feature and it is a deliberate consequence. An invite flow creates an identity that did not come from the customer’s directory, which means the customer cannot revoke it by deprovisioning in their IdP. For a customer whose entire reason for buying this is provable control over who can reach their data, an identity outside their directory is a hole in the product.

INFERENCE The cost is real and worth naming: external collaboration — contractors, vendors, customers raising service desk tickets — becomes an identity-federation problem rather than an invite. That is a genuine product limitation, not an oversight, and saying so demonstrates you can see a trade rather than a gap.

🎯 The modelling principle

The control plane stores pointers and versions; the data plane stores content. Any field you are tempted to add to the control plane should be tested against one question: would a customer object to Atlassian holding this centrally? If yes, it belongs inside the boundary and the control plane gets an identifier instead.

Regions and data residency

The most important fact here is a negative one, and getting it wrong in an interview is worse than not knowing it.

⚠ The claim not to make
“Isolated Cloud gives you data sovereignty.”

CONFIRMED Atlassian states plainly that Isolated Cloud does not support data sovereignty, and that it follows the same law-enforcement guidelines as commercial cloud.

These are different properties. Residency is where bytes sit — you pick a region at onboarding, default us-east-1. Sovereignty is whose law reaches them, which depends on the operator’s jurisdiction, not the data centre’s. A US-headquartered operator remains subject to US legal process wherever the disk is.

Isolation does not change that, and a customer buying AIC for sovereignty has bought the wrong product. Being able to draw that distinction cleanly is a strong signal in a security-architecture conversation.

Why residency is architectural rather than a setting

INFERENCE A region choice sounds like a dropdown and it constrains the design in four ways:

  • Backups and DR must stay in-region, or the residency promise is broken by the recovery path — which is exactly where such promises usually break, because DR is designed later and by different people.
  • Every dependency must exist in that region. One control-plane service available only in us-east-1 silently makes every environment depend on it.
  • Cross-region replication becomes a policy decision, not an availability optimisation. The cheap way to survive a regional failure is the thing residency forbids.
  • Support tooling is in scope. If an engineer’s debugging session pulls data to another region, residency is broken by the operational path even when the storage path is correct.

Apps, integrations, and the egress consent model

Isolation is hardest on the ecosystem, because an app’s whole purpose is often to talk to something outside.

CapabilityStatusDetail
Forge appsSupportedCONFIRMED “Isolated Cloud supports all Forge apps.”
Connect & Connect-on-ForgeNot supportedCONFIRMED Stated explicitly.
App distributionSeparateCONFIRMED A separate App ID for Isolated Cloud; one codebase with environment variables is the recommended approach.

A documented inconsistency — know it, and handle it well. The admin-facing egress documentation says “app egress is disabled by default” with per-remote consent. The developer-facing publishing documentation says “there are no platform-enforced egress restrictions specific to Isolated Cloud” and frames it as customer discretion.

INFERENCE The most likely reconciliation is that these describe different layers: the platform does not forbid an app from declaring an external remote, and the customer’s environment denies traffic to it until an admin consents. If asked, say exactly that — identify the tension, offer the reading, and mark it as something you would confirm. Noticing a contradiction between two docs is a better signal than smoothly reciting one of them.

Why this is really a dependency-graph problem

INFERENCE A customer does not adopt “Jira”. They adopt Jira plus eleven apps, six integrations and a hundred automation rules, and that graph is what actually has to move. Isolation cuts every edge in it that leaves the boundary.

So the migration blocker is rarely data volume. It is a transitive closure: this team depends on an app, which depends on a Connect module that does not exist here, or on a remote the security team will not approve. The unit of migration readiness is the dependency graph, not the dataset — and that reframing is worth more in an interview than any throughput number.

12

The Staff-level problems

Seven problems that genuinely need someone operating above a single team. For each: why it is hard, why the simple answer fails, and what Staff-level ownership looks like as distinct from team-level implementation.

1Strong isolation with a shared control plane. Operate an environment you have deliberately limited your own access to.Open

Why it is hard. Every operational capability you normally rely on — read the database to diagnose, tail logs, reproduce with real data — is exactly what isolation is sold as preventing. You have to run the environment while being unable to look inside it in the ways you are used to.

Why the simple answer fails. “Give operators break-glass access” works technically and destroys the product claim if it is routine. The value being sold is that Atlassian cannot casually see customer content.

The shape of the answer. Push diagnosis to signals that are not content: structured metrics, traces and log lines whose fields are classified and cleared by the taxonomy. Make break-glass exist, but make it rare, time-boxed, customer-visible and audited. The design goal is that the common case never needs it.

Staff-level: defining what “debuggable without content access” means, and holding that line when a team asks for a shortcut during an incident.
2Provisioning an environment at all. A dedicated OU, accounts, VPC, edge, domain, keys and products — repeatably, on a sales timeline.Open

Why it is hard. It is a distributed transaction across systems that do not share one: AWS Organizations, DNS, certificates, KMS grants in a customer’s account, product provisioning, identity configuration. Any step can fail, and several are not idempotent by nature.

Why the simple answer fails. A runbook works for the first ten customers and becomes the constraint at a hundred — both in time and in drift, because no two manually-built environments end up identical.

The shape of the answer. Declarative desired state plus a reconciling controller, exactly as in D18: the environment spec is data, a controller drives reality toward it, every step is idempotent, and partial failure resumes rather than restarts. Drift detection then becomes the same mechanism as provisioning.

Staff-level: choosing declarative reconciliation over imperative orchestration, and defining the environment spec that becomes the contract between every team that touches it.
3Deploying to a fleet of isolated environments. One platform change, hundreds of separate AWS accounts.Open

Why it is hard. In multi-tenant cloud a fix reaches everyone at once. Here every environment is a separate deployment target that can be at a different version, in a different region, with different products enabled. Version skew becomes permanent rather than transient.

Why the simple answer fails. Deploying everywhere simultaneously gives a blast radius of the entire customer base. Deploying one at a time takes longer than the gap between releases.

The shape of the answer. Waves with bake time and automatic halt on regression — the D18 pattern — plus a hard rule that every change is backward compatible for at least one version, because you cannot assume the fleet is ever uniform.

Staff-level: setting the compatibility rule and the wave policy, then enforcing them across teams who each believe their change is the safe exception.
4Migrating customers in. Terabytes of Jira and Confluence data from a Data Center instance that is still being used.Open

Why it is hard. The source keeps changing while you copy it, the destination is behind an isolation boundary with a different URL model and default-deny egress, and identity has to be reconciled against an IdP because CONFIRMED non-IdP users cannot be invited.

Why the simple answer fails. A long write freeze is not available to a bank running change management on a global Jira. Export-and-import is a single unresumable operation at a size where failure is likely.

The shape of the answer. Bulk copy, then incremental catch-up, then a short freeze for the final delta — covered in depth in §13–§16.

Staff-level: choosing the consistency model and the cutover semantics, and being the person who says how long the freeze actually needs to be.
5Keeping the ecosystem working. Apps, integrations and automations that assumed multi-tenancy and an open network.Open

Why it is hard. It is not your code. Marketplace partners must build and certify a separate version, and every customer integration that hardcoded atlassian.net or an outbound call now fails.

Why the simple answer fails. You cannot fix it centrally, and you cannot ship the product without an ecosystem — an enterprise Jira with no apps is not a migration destination.

The shape of the answer. Make the platform difference small and mechanical — CONFIRMED one codebase, environment variables, a separate App ID — so the partner cost is a build rather than a rewrite. Then treat it as a dependency-graph problem per customer during assessment.

Staff-level: owning the compatibility contract with an ecosystem you do not control, and deciding what the platform absorbs versus what partners must change.
6Preventing accidental egress across a codebase where thousands of engineers add fields to cross-service messages.Open

Why it is hard. The failure is silent and asymmetric: nothing breaks when customer data leaks, and the blast radius is the product’s entire value proposition.

Why the simple answer fails. A guideline is enforced by whoever reviews the pull request. Across a codebase this size it will be violated, and discovered externally.

The shape of the answer. CONFIRMED Classify every field in a data taxonomy and enforce it with code-based policy in the Transit Policy Manager, with a proxy that obfuscates anything not explicitly cleared. Unclassified must fail closed — a new field with no classification should break the build, not default to allowed.

Staff-level: making “unclassified fails closed” the rule, and absorbing the organisational friction of every team now having to classify their fields.
7Supporting a customer whose data you cannot read. “Jira is slow”, and the usual debugging path is closed.Open

Why it is hard. Normal support attaches a ticket to an account, reads recent activity, reproduces the issue. Here CONFIRMED only an email address is persisted commercially; display name, account ID and locale are transit-only.

Why the simple answer fails. Asking the customer to paste logs moves the leak rather than removing it, and it makes support slower exactly when it matters most.

The shape of the answer. Rich content-free telemetry — latency by endpoint, by shard, by dependency — plus customer-side tooling that lets them gather the detail and share only what they choose.

Staff-level: specifying the observability contract early, because retrofitting content-free diagnosability into a system designed for content access is close to impossible.

The guarantees, stated honestly

Exactly-once delivery across a network is not achievable — the same result as D6 and D26. What you can build is at-least-once transfer with idempotent application, which yields exactly-once effects.

The mechanism is the ID map. Every entity is written with a key derived from its source identifier, so re-applying a unit updates the same destination record rather than creating a second one. Duplicates are prevented by the write being idempotent, not by transfer being reliable.

The questionThe answer
How do you prevent data loss?Checkpoint only after the destination confirms. A crash between write and checkpoint redoes the write, which is safe because it is idempotent. The reverse ordering — checkpoint then write — loses data silently.
How do you prevent duplicates?The ID map plus deterministic destination keys. Not by trying to make transfer exactly-once.
Out-of-order events?Order within an entity by source version or timestamp and ignore anything older than what is already applied. Across entities, enforce order by dependency in the scheduler — a comment cannot land before its work item.
Same event twice?Idempotent write. It is a no-op. This is the normal case, not an error path.
A delete arrives before an update?The genuinely nasty one. Apply deletes as tombstones carrying a version, and reject any update older than the tombstone — otherwise a late update resurrects deleted data, which for a bank is a compliance incident rather than a bug.
Source changes mid-migration?Expected. That is what SYNCING exists for. Bulk reads a snapshot so it is internally consistent; incremental closes the gap from the watermark.
Worker crashes after copying, before checkpointing?The unit is redispatched and redone. Costs duplicate work; costs no correctness. This is precisely why the ordering is write-then-checkpoint.
🎯 The one-line version

“We do not try to make the transfer exactly-once, because you cannot. We make the transfer at-least-once and the write idempotent, and we checkpoint only after the destination has confirmed. That turns every crash into repeated work rather than lost or duplicated data.”

Why “retry everything” is dangerous

Retrying a permanent error is worse than failing. A malformed entity retried forever consumes a worker permanently, hides the real error behind noise, and at scale turns a struggling destination into a failed one — the retries become the outage, exactly as in D6.

So classify before retrying:

  • Retryable — timeouts, 5xx, throttling, connection resets. Exponential backoff with jitter, because a failure synchronises every worker and un-jittered retries arrive as a wave.
  • Permanent — 4xx, schema violations, unsupported entities. Straight to the DLQ with the reason. Never retried.
  • Ambiguous — a timeout after the request may have been applied. Retry, and rely on the write being idempotent. This is the case that makes idempotency mandatory rather than nice.

And a circuit breaker per destination: if the failure rate crosses a threshold, stop sending entirely rather than continuing to hammer something already unhealthy.

Cutover, and the hard rollback question

Cutover weekend
The only window where users are affected.
  1. Friday 18:00 — write freeze on source. Enforced technically, not by announcement. Read-only mode, so people can still find things.
  2. Final delta. Small by construction — weeks of incremental sync made it minutes rather than days. This is the number the whole design optimised.
  3. Validate. Counts per project and space, checksums on a sample, permission spot-checks, cross-link integrity. Against agreed thresholds decided in advance, not judged in the moment.
  4. Switch. DNS and identity point at the isolated environment. Source stays up, read-only.
  5. Smoke test with a named group of real users on real workflows, before anyone else is let in.
  6. Sunday — unfreeze. Users work in the destination. Elevated support for a week.
Every step before Friday was reversible. From the moment users write to the destination, the cost of reversing starts climbing — which is why the go/no-go gate is before the switch, not after.
“What if we find a serious problem after users have written to the new environment?”

The honest answer is that full rollback stops being attractive very quickly, and saying so is better than pretending otherwise. Application rollback is easy — redeploy the previous version. Data rollback is not, because reverting to the pre-cutover source discards every edit users have made since. Two days in, that is two days of a bank’s work.

So there are three real options, and the third is usually correct:

  • Roll back within hours — viable only in a short window, and only if you decided the threshold in advance. Users lose what they did.
  • Reverse-migrate — move the new writes back. Technically possible, and you are now running the same hard migration under incident pressure, in the direction nobody rehearsed.
  • Forward-fix — keep the destination authoritative and repair. Almost always right, and it is why the source is retained read-only: as reference for repair rather than as a rollback target.

The Staff-level move is to set the rollback expiry before cutover and say out loud that after it, the plan is forward-fix. A team that has not decided this in advance will try to roll back at hour 40 and make things considerably worse.

Operating it

18

Fleet, security, observability, DR

Deploying a breaking change to a thousand isolated environments

The question you are most likely to be asked, and the answer starts by refusing the premise.

You do not deploy a breaking change. You split it into compatible steps — the expand-then-contract discipline from D18. First ship code that understands both the old and new form and deploy it everywhere. Then migrate the data. Then, in a later release, remove the old path. Three deployments, each individually reversible, instead of one that is not.

INFERENCE On top of that: waves with bake time, automatic halt on regression, canary environments that opt in, and a hard rule that every version is compatible with the one before it — because with hundreds of separate accounts the fleet is never uniform. Version skew is the steady state, not a transient.

And one property that isolation gives you for free: per-customer rollback. In multi-tenant cloud you cannot roll back one customer. Here you can, because they are a separate deployment target. That is worth naming as one of the few operational benefits of the model.

Operating without violating isolation

The central security tension of the whole product. Four mechanisms, and the first is the one that matters most:

  • Make the common case need no access. If diagnosis routinely requires reading customer data, no access policy will hold — it will be worn down by every incident. Invest in content-free telemetry so the normal path never asks.
  • Break-glass as an exception: time-boxed, justified, approved, fully audited, and INFERENCE ideally visible to the customer. Access that the customer can see is access that stays rare.
  • Customer-held keys CONFIRMED — CMK in the customer’s AWS account. The strongest control here, because it is not a promise: revoking the grant is something the customer can do without asking.
  • Automation over humans. Every operation that runs as code rather than as a person is one that cannot read anything it was not written to read.

“A customer says Jira is slow. How do you debug it?”

  1. Scope it. Everyone or some people? All operations or one? Since when? This needs no customer data and eliminates most hypotheses.
  2. Check the environment as a unit. It is a dedicated account — so unlike multi-tenant, resource exhaustion is their traffic. Compute saturation, database CPU and connections, storage throughput, queue depth.
  3. Split by endpoint, then by dependency. Uniform slowness points at a shared resource — usually the database. One endpoint points at one code path or one expensive query.
  4. Check the boundary. A new egress rule, a blocked call being retried, an Isolation Gateway call on a hot path. These are failure modes this architecture has and normal cloud does not.
  5. Compare against the fleet. The single most useful signal: is this environment slow, or is this version slow everywhere? Content-free, and it distinguishes a customer problem from a platform problem in one query.
  6. If it is genuinely their data shape — a project with 400,000 issues, a filter with no index — that is where customer-side tooling matters: let them run the diagnostic and share the result, rather than you reaching in.

Disaster recovery, and the residency constraint

CONFIRMED Backup and restore are available by default, and uptime SLAs match Cloud Enterprise. INFERENCE The design tension worth raising: the cheapest way to survive a regional failure is cross-region replication, and that is exactly what a residency commitment restricts. So regional DR becomes a negotiated property per customer rather than a platform default — and a customer who chose strict residency has implicitly chosen a longer RTO. That trade should be explicit in the contract, not discovered during a regional outage.

The distinctive failure mode here is key loss. If the customer’s KMS key is deleted or the grant revoked, data at rest is unrecoverable — and that is the feature working as designed. It makes key lifecycle a joint operational responsibility, which is unusual and worth naming.

Interviewer pushback

Twelve questions a Staff+ interviewer actually asks, with a short answer, the follow-up you should expect, and how to go deeper.

1“Why isn’t a separate VPC enough?”Answer

Short. A VPC is a network boundary; this is an operational one. The account, the edge, the domain, the keys and the operating model all change, and four of those five are not network concerns.

Follow-up: “Which of those actually matters most?” The account boundary and the keys. The account is where AWS actually enforces IAM, quotas and blast radius — a VPC inside someone else’s account still sits inside their IAM. And customer-held keys change the trust model from a promise into a control the customer can exercise without asking.

Name the operational half. Everyone gets the network half.
2“Why is the control plane shared? Isn’t that a hole in the isolation story?”Answer

Short. Because isolating it would mean running hundreds of copies of the hardest parts of Atlassian, each drifting to a different version. A security patch would become hundreds of projects.

Follow-up: “So what stops customer data reaching it?” Two enforced asymmetries. Inbound is restricted by endpoint — only allowlisted APIs are reachable through the Isolation Gateway. Outbound is restricted by field — every field is classified and the Isolated Context Gateway obfuscates anything not cleared. The control plane knows an environment exists and what version it runs. It does not know what is inside it.

Do not defend the shared control plane as safe. Explain the two different enforcement models that make it safe.
3“What happens if the control plane goes down?”Answer

Short. Customers keep working. Serving does not depend on it — compute, data, edge and identity are all inside the boundary.

Follow-up: “Even login?” Yes, and that is deliberate. Identity processing and session state stay inside, and users authenticate against their own IdP. Had auth depended on id.atlassian.com, isolation would have bought a shared failure domain rather than removing one. What stops is provisioning, deployments, new sites, billing and reaching Marketplace and Support.

The interesting part is why identity was kept inside. That is the design decision; availability is the consequence.
4“How do you operate an environment you can’t look inside?”Answer

Short. Make the common case need no access: content-free telemetry rich enough to diagnose without reading data. Break-glass exists, is time-boxed and audited, and is rare by design.

Follow-up: “Rare in practice, or rare in policy?” The honest answer is that if diagnosis routinely needs content access, the policy will be worn down by incidents — so the only durable control is investment in telemetry, not in approval workflow. The metric to watch is break-glass invocations per month; if it is not falling, the observability is inadequate and the policy is theatre.

Treat break-glass frequency as an observability metric, not a compliance one.
5“How do you migrate terabytes while people are still working?”Answer

Short. Bulk copy from a snapshot while the source stays live, then repeated incremental passes until the remaining delta is small, then a short freeze for the final delta.

Follow-up: “How short is short?” That is the number the whole design optimises. It is set by the delta, not by total size — which is why the incremental phase runs for weeks. For a 6 TB instance, bulk is a day or two of actual transfer; the six-week timeline is assessment, app readiness, IdP reconciliation and rehearsal, not copying.

Say that bandwidth is not the constraint. It shows you sized it instead of assuming.
6“Why not CDC? Why not dual-write?”Answer

Short. CDC on a decade-old Data Center schema is a larger build than the migration and couples you to internal table structures across many versions. Dual-write means every write must succeed in two systems with different semantics, which either blocks the source on the destination or silently diverges.

Follow-up: “Dual-write would give you instant rollback though.” It would, and it moves the failure into the live write path of a system the customer depends on today. You would be risking their working environment to de-risk the cutover. Application-level incremental sync keeps all the risk on the migration side, where failure is a delay rather than an outage.

Reject dual-write on where the risk lands, not on complexity.
7“What happens at 95% completion and it stops moving?”Answer

Short. Almost always a small number of pathological items — a multi-gigabyte attachment, an issue with 10,000 comments, an entity hitting an unexpected limit. Not a systemic problem.

Follow-up: “So what do you do?” DLQ them with the reason, finish the other 5%, and handle them as a named list. The failure to avoid is blocking the whole migration on twelve items, or retrying them forever so the stall looks like progress. This is also why progress must be reported as units remaining rather than a percentage — a percentage hides a long tail.

Report units remaining, not percent complete. Percentages conceal exactly this.
8“How do you guarantee no data loss and no duplicates?”Answer

Short. You do not make the transfer exactly-once, because you cannot. At-least-once transfer, idempotent writes keyed through an ID map, and checkpoints written only after the destination confirms.

Follow-up: “What if the ID map is lost?” That is the genuinely unrecoverable failure, and worth calling out: without it, re-running creates duplicates instead of updates. It is the one component that needs ledger-grade durability and backup. Everything else in the platform can be rebuilt by re-running.

Naming your single unrecoverable component is a stronger signal than claiming nothing can fail.
9“A delete arrives before the update it supersedes. What happens?”Answer

Short. Deletes are applied as tombstones carrying a version, and any update older than the tombstone is rejected.

Follow-up: “Why not just apply whatever arrives last?” Because a late update would resurrect deleted data. For a regulated customer, content reappearing after deletion is a compliance incident, not a bug — they may have deleted it because they were required to. The asymmetry matters: a rejected stale update loses nothing, and a resurrection is unrecoverable trust damage.

Argue it from the consequence asymmetry, not from ordering theory.
10“How do you deploy a breaking change to a thousand isolated environments?”Answer

Short. You do not. You split it into compatible steps: deploy code that reads both forms, migrate the data, then remove the old path in a later release. Three reversible deployments instead of one that is not.

Follow-up: “And if a team insists their change can’t be split?” That is the Staff conversation, and it is usually a schema change where splitting is inconvenient rather than impossible. The rule has to hold, because with hundreds of separate accounts the fleet is never uniform — version skew is the steady state. One exception makes the compatibility guarantee meaningless for everyone.

Isolation does give one gift here: per-customer rollback, which multi-tenant cannot do.
11“How do you prevent data exfiltration by a misbehaving service or app?”Answer

Short. Egress is default-deny and enforced by code-based policy, with every field classified by a taxonomy and a proxy that obfuscates anything not explicitly cleared. Apps need per-remote admin consent, revocable and audit-logged.

Follow-up: “What about a new field someone adds next week?” This is the important case. Unclassified must fail closed — a field with no classification should break the build rather than default to allowed. A taxonomy that fails open is a taxonomy that degrades to nothing within a year, because the default is what actually governs a large codebase.

The defaults are the policy. Everything else is documentation.
12“Doesn’t this just give customers sovereignty?”Answer

Short. No, and Atlassian says so explicitly — Isolated Cloud does not support data sovereignty.

Follow-up: “Why not? The data is in their region.” Residency is where the bytes sit; sovereignty is whose law reaches them, and that follows the operator’s jurisdiction rather than the disk’s. A US-headquartered operator remains subject to US legal process wherever the data centre is. Isolation and dedicated keys do not change that, and a customer buying this for sovereignty has bought the wrong product.

Being able to say what a product does not do is a security-architecture signal.

The story, at four lengths

Before using any of these: they are written in the first person because that is how you will say them, and they describe the system. Replace any ownership claim with what you actually did. Where a number appears, use your real one or say you would need to check — an invented metric is the fastest way to lose a Staff+ interview.

60 seconds — “your most impactful project”

“Atlassian had a segment of enterprise customers — banks, critical infrastructure — whose compliance rules forbid shared infrastructure outright. They could not move to cloud at all, so they stayed on Data Center, which has an end-of-support date. Isolated Cloud is the answer: each customer gets dedicated AWS accounts, their own VPC, their own domain and edge, and their own encryption keys, with egress blocked by default.

The interesting constraint is that the control plane stays shared — provisioning, deployment and fleet operations run once for everyone — because duplicating those per customer means running hundreds of copies of Atlassian. So all the hard engineering is at that boundary: operating an environment you have deliberately limited your own access to, and moving customer data into it without a write freeze anyone could accept.”

2 minutes — add the shape

Take the 60-second version and add three things: the two gateways and why they are asymmetric (endpoint allowlisting inbound, field-level obfuscation outbound); the identity inversion (the isolated environment is the OAuth provider for Atlassian, not the other way round, which is why a control-plane outage does not log anyone out); and the migration shape (bulk, then incremental, then a short freeze — optimising the delta rather than the throughput).

One-page cheat sheet

What it is

Atlassian Cloud deployed one customer at a time. Dedicated AWS OU and accounts, VPC, edge, domain, firewall, customer-managed keys. Jira, JSM, Confluence. Cloud Enterprise features plus Guard Premium. Available 2026.

The one idea

Dedicated data plane, shared control plane. Everything touching customer data is isolated; everything operating the software is shared. Every hard problem is downstream of that.

The boundary

Inbound: Isolation Gateway, allowlisted endpoints only. Outbound: Controlled & Monitored Egress + Isolated Context Gateway, field-level obfuscation, governed by the Transit Policy Manager. Default deny.

Identity

All auth inside the boundary. SSO only; no Google Workspace IdP; no non-IdP invites. The isolated env acts as OAuth/OIDC provider for id.atlassian.com. Mints a User Context Token.

Endpoints

<site>.<org>.atlassian-isolated.net, admin on .com. Per-customer base domain. Three differences from normal cloud: TLD, an extra label, and no shared host — which is why integrations break.

Apps

Forge yes, Connect and Connect-on-Forge no. Separate App ID, one codebase, env vars. App egress off until admin consents per remote. Third-party automation blocked by default. Catalogue is a subset.

Migration

Cloud Migration Assistants, EAP. Assess → Plan → Prep → Test → Migrate → Post. Bulk → incremental → short freeze. At-least-once + idempotent writes + ID map. Checkpoint after confirm.

Do not claim

No data sovereignty — Atlassian says so. No AWS VPN. 150 sites per org. Don’t call it private cloud, Government Cloud, or single-tenant (Atlassian says single-customer). Don’t invent internals.

Twenty questions, twenty short answers

QuestionAnswer
What is AIC?Atlassian Cloud deployed per customer: dedicated AWS accounts, VPC, edge, domain and keys, with egress denied by default. Control plane stays shared.
Why does it exist?A segment whose rules forbid shared infrastructure could not move to cloud at all. Data Center support ends March 2029, so they had a deadline and no destination.
Single-tenant?Atlassian says single-customer. One customer, potentially many sites inside their environment. The boundary is the customer, not the site.
Why not just a VPC?A VPC is one layer. The account boundary, dedicated edge and domain, customer-held keys and the operating model all change too.
Why is the control plane shared?Isolating it means hundreds of drifting copies of the hardest parts of the company. One patch would become hundreds of projects.
What crosses the boundary?Non-UGC, non-personal metadata. Transit-only, persisted where justified, or TTL’d as short as five minutes. Everything else is obfuscated.
How is that enforced?Code-based policy in the Transit Policy Manager, over a field-level data taxonomy, through the Isolated Context Gateway.
Control plane down?Customers keep working, including login, because identity is inside. Provisioning, deploys, billing and Marketplace stop.
One environment fails?One customer. Separate account, separate capacity. That is the blast-radius benefit of the model.
How does login work?Edge → ingress gateway → auth service → customer IdP → User Context Token. All inside. Commercial services federate inward.
Why are URLs different?Dedicated edge and per-customer base domain. Breaks hostname assumptions, discovery and reachability — three separate failures.
What about apps?Forge only. Separate App ID, one codebase. Egress needs per-remote admin consent. Catalogue is a subset, and that is a real migration blocker.
How do you migrate?Bulk from a snapshot, repeated incremental passes, then a short freeze for a deliberately small delta.
How long?Bulk for 6 TB is a day or two of transfer. The six-week timeline is assessment, app readiness, IdP reconciliation and rehearsal.
No duplicates, no loss?At-least-once transfer, idempotent writes via an ID map, checkpoint only after the destination confirms.
Stuck at 95%?A few pathological items. DLQ them, finish, handle separately. Report units remaining rather than a percentage.
Rollback after cutover?Viable for hours, then forward-fix, because reverting discards everything users have written since. Set the expiry in advance.
Deploy to 1,000 environments?Never ship a breaking change — expand, migrate, contract. Waves, bake time, automatic halt. Per-customer rollback is possible here.
Operate without seeing data?Content-free telemetry rich enough for the common case; break-glass rare, time-boxed, audited. Track invocations as an observability metric.
